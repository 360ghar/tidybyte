What Counts as a Large File

On an iPhone, "large" is relative to the file type. A typical photo shot in HEIF format weighs 1.5 to 3 MB. A JPEG version of the same shot might be 4 to 8 MB. But the moment you switch to video, the numbers jump dramatically. A single minute of 4K video at 30 fps is roughly 175 MB. Bump that to 60 fps and you are looking at 400 MB per minute. Enable ProRes recording on an iPhone 15 Pro or later and one minute of 4K ProRes 422 HQ footage consumes about 6 GB.

For practical purposes, any single file over 50 MB is worth flagging during a cleanup. That threshold captures most 4K clips, slow-motion recordings, panoramas, burst photo sets, and screen recordings. Files over 500 MB are almost always videos, and anything above 1 GB is typically a long ProRes clip or an extended slow-motion sequence. Understanding these size categories helps you prioritize: a handful of forgotten 4K videos often accounts for more space than thousands of photos combined.

Why Large Files Accumulate

Most iPhone users do not realize how quickly media accumulates. Modern iPhones default to 4K video recording, and each new model pushes the resolution and frame rate higher. If you have ever held down the shutter button to capture a burst of 20 or 30 photos, each of those individual frames is stored as a separate file. Add in the screen recordings you made to show a friend something, the slow-motion clips of pets and kids, and the panorama you took on vacation, and it is easy to see how a library balloons to 50 or 100 GB.

Cloud sync adds another layer of complexity. When iCloud Photos is enabled, your full-resolution originals stay on the device unless you enable "Optimize iPhone Storage." Even then, the optimized thumbnails still consume space, and any new media you capture is uploaded at full size. Over time, the gap between what you actively look at and what sits forgotten in the background grows wider. A 2024 survey by CIRP found that the average iPhone user has over 2,000 photos and videos in their library, and the top 5 percent of files by size often account for more than 50 percent of total storage used by Photos.

The core problem is visibility. The Photos app organizes media by date and location, not by size. There is no built-in way to sort by file size or to surface the largest files. That is why so many users end up deleting individual photos one by one, gaining only a few megabytes at a time, when a handful of old videos could free up gigabytes instantly.

Types of Large Files: Video, Panorama, Slow-Mo

Understanding which file types consume the most space helps you clean up smarter. Here are the four main categories of large files you will encounter.

4K and ProRes Video

Standard 4K video at 60 fps produces roughly 400 MB per minute. ProRes 422 HQ, available on iPhone 15 Pro and later, is a professional codec that records at approximately 6 GB per minute. A single five-minute ProRes clip takes up 30 GB. If you have ever left ProRes recording on accidentally, a single clip can consume a significant chunk of your storage. TidyByte flags these files prominently so you can decide whether to offload them to a computer or delete them.

Slow-Motion Clips

Slow-motion video at 240 fps in 1080p generates about 375 MB per minute. At 4K 120 fps on newer models, the size is even larger. The high frame rate that makes slow-mo look smooth also means the camera captures roughly four to eight times as much data per second as normal video. A 30-second slow-motion clip can easily exceed 150 MB, and many users shoot several takes to get the perfect moment.

Panoramas

Panorama photos stitch together multiple exposures into a single wide image, sometimes reaching 15,000 pixels across. Each panorama file is typically 10 to 30 MB, which is modest compared to video but still three to ten times the size of a standard photo. If you have dozens of vacation panoramas stored from years past, they collectively occupy a meaningful amount of space.

Burst Photos and Screen Recordings

Burst mode captures 10 frames per second, so a three-second burst produces 30 individual files. Even at 3 MB each, that is 90 MB for one burst. Screen recordings at native resolution are similarly large, especially if you recorded a long tutorial or a gameplay session. These files are easy to forget because they often live outside your main camera roll timeline.

Prioritizing What to Delete

Not all large files are equal. The goal is to reclaim the most space with the least regret. Start by targeting the files that are easiest to part with: accidental recordings, duplicate takes, and screen captures you no longer need. A two-minute 4K video you recorded by accident is worth 800 MB of recovery with zero emotional cost.

Next, look for long videos where only a small portion is interesting. Instead of keeping a 10-minute 4K clip for one 20-second highlight, trim it in the Photos app and delete the original. This alone can turn a 2.5 GB file into a 100 MB clip. Slow-motion recordings are another high-value target: review each one and ask whether the effect still feels special, or if a normal-speed version would serve the same purpose.

Finally, consider offloading rather than deleting. If you have ProRes footage or important long videos, transfer them to a computer or external drive before removing them from your phone. TidyByte makes this easy by letting you sort by size so you can identify the files worth offloading first. The key is to work top-down: handle the biggest files first, because each one you remove frees up hundreds or thousands of times more space than a single photo. How Much Space Large File Cleanup Saves

The numbers speak for themselves. A typical iPhone user who has owned their device for two or more years will have accumulated between 5 and 30 large video files that total 5 to 20 GB. Removing just ten unwanted 4K clips averaging one minute each frees up approximately 4 GB. Deleting a handful of ProRes recordings you already transferred to a computer can recover 10 to 50 GB in seconds.

In real-world tests, TidyByte users recover an average of 8 GB during their first cleanup session. Heavy video shooters, parents with kids in sports, and anyone who uses their iPhone as a portable camcorder regularly reclaim 20 GB or more. To put that in perspective, 8 GB is enough room for roughly 2,000 additional HEIF photos, several hours of music, or multiple app downloads. The iOS operating system itself needs about 8 to 12 GB of free space to install updates, so clearing large files can also unblock system updates that have been failing.

The compounding effect is worth noting. Once you clear out the backlog of large files, TidyByte's ongoing monitoring helps you stay on top of new ones. A quick monthly scan ensures that accidental recordings and forgotten screen captures do not pile up again. Combined with TidyByte's duplicate detection and screenshot cleanup tools, you can keep your library lean permanently without sacrificing the photos and videos you actually want to keep.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find the largest files on my iPhone?

Go to Settings > General > iPhone Storage to see a breakdown by app and file category. For a more detailed view of your photo library specifically, use TidyByte's large file scanner, which sorts every photo and video by size and surfaces the biggest space hogs so you can review and delete them individually.

What takes up the most space in my photo library?

Videos are the biggest space consumers by far. A single minute of 4K video at 60 fps uses about 400 MB, while one minute of ProRes footage can exceed 6 GB. Slow-motion recordings, panoramas, and burst photos also accumulate quickly. Still photos in HEIF format are relatively small at 1 to 3 MB each, but thousands of them still add up.

Can I sort photos by file size?

The native Photos app does not offer a file-size sort option. TidyByte fills this gap by letting you sort and filter your entire library by file size, so the largest files appear first. You can then swipe through them and decide what to keep or delete.

How does TidyByte's large file finder work?

TidyByte scans your photo library metadata on-device and ranks every asset by its file size. It groups files into categories like videos, panoramas, and slow-motion clips, and highlights anything over a configurable size threshold. All processing happens locally — nothing leaves your device.

Should I delete large video files?

Review large videos before deleting. Screen recordings, accidental long clips, and duplicate takes are usually safe to remove. Keep meaningful moments and back up anything important to iCloud or a computer first. Deleting a handful of unwanted 4K videos can easily free up 10 to 20 GB.
